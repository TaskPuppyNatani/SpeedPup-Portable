# Phase 3C.2 — Full diagnostics

Full requires confirmation and normally takes about 1–3 minutes. It performs the Standard baseline ICMP window and DNS checks, then separate download and upload traffic stages, then three recovery ICMP probes. Select Light (24 MiB), Standard (128 MiB, default), or Extended (256 MiB) as the maximum payload per direction; the total maximum is twice that amount, and actual consumption can be lower. Server discovery, endpoint probes, transport overhead and existing traffic are additional. Load starts with one LibreSpeed connection and ramps toward three at half-second intervals while budget remains, for up to eight seconds. Workers share the budget, including reservations for in-flight I/O, so an idle or slow worker does not strand a fixed portion of the allowance. Each stage has a 35-second supervising deadline. The child owns a shorter 28-second budget — at most 12 seconds for endpoint discovery and selection, up to eight seconds of transfer, and one three-second cleanup window shared by all load workers rather than one per worker — so it reports a result before the supervisor would have to kill it. Bounded server selection runs candidate latency probes on daemon worker threads, stops waiting for slower candidates once at least one validated candidate has succeeded and a two-second settle window elapses (or returns the lowest-latency already-validated candidate if the discovery allowance expires), and cancels late resolver completions before opening stray sockets. When download completes cleanly without unrecovered endpoint failure, upload reuses that validated server (re-checking URL/origin validation and per-connection public-address and TLS validation) instead of repeating server-list retrieval and selection; if download does not yield a cleanly reusable server, upload runs bounded discovery independently. Endpoint name resolution and connection setup are bounded inside that budget instead of left to the resolver, because `getaddrinfo` ignores the two-second socket timeout; socket I/O keeps the two-second timeout. A child process isolates potentially blocking DNS and network work and is killed and reaped on cancellation or deadline. Reporting distinguishes an expired supervising deadline, a child that could not start, a child that ended before reporting results, an endpoint failure, discovery exceeding its allowance, and load workers that did not stop within the cleanup window; a bounded, sanitized excerpt of the child's stderr is retained for the start and early-exit cases. Quick and Standard do not start this process or generate load traffic.

Baseline latency is the arithmetic mean of successful ICMP replies in the unloaded Standard window. Loaded latency is the arithmetic mean of ICMP replies whose entire probe is bracketed by recent increasing transfer counters. The chart uses real elapsed and wall-clock sample times; failed probes and periods without observed traffic are gaps. The first and last observed transfer-counter updates delimit the displayed load bands, so their edges have approximately 0.2-second sampling resolution and do not establish the exact first or last network packet. Recovery probes are shown separately and do not change the baseline or grade.

An attempt to open a new connection may be cut short by the planned end of the load window; that boundary is not classified as an endpoint failure. A setup timeout that expires independently before the window ends remains an endpoint failure. Sockets returned after a setup attempt was abandoned are closed. After the child reports its result, the supervisor allows a short exit grace period and reaps a child that remains alive; such an unclean result cannot qualify for grading. An in-progress ping can delay recognition of the result and the start of that grace period.

For each direction, additional latency is `max(0, loaded mean - baseline mean)`. Baseline and each load direction require at least three valid replies and at least 75% of attempted probes valid. Probes begin only while recent transfer counters are increasing and before the load stage ends; every such attempted probe counts toward coverage, including probes that could not be associated with a transfer interval. A load direction also requires at least 1 MiB transferred, at least five seconds of observed increasing byte counters, and a clean load process. Transfer errors disqualify the direction, except for the individually qualified setup-timeout case described below. Upload additionally requires at least 1 MiB in complete requests accepted by the endpoint. Otherwise the grade is unavailable. A directional latency summary may still be shown when that direction has enough valid probes, even if the load itself was insufficient for a grade. Results distinguish budget exhaustion, endpoint failure, insufficient sustained transfer, and insufficient latency samples. Any budget can be reached before enough coverage is collected on a fast enough connection; choose a larger budget if appropriate. The estimate uses the worse of the two additional latencies, with inclusive boundaries:

| Worst increase | Grade |
| --- | --- |
| 0–5 ms | A+ |
| >5–30 ms | A |
| >30–60 ms | B |
| >60–200 ms | C |
| >200–400 ms | D |
| >400 ms | F |

These are SpeedPup thresholds, not a claim of certification by another testing service. Transfer activity and the minimum thresholds do **not** prove the user's internet access link was saturated. Server performance, routing, byte caps, background traffic, Wi-Fi, ICMP filtering or rate limiting, and a remote test bottleneck can all affect the result. In particular, a good estimated grade can understate bufferbloat if the actual bottleneck was not sufficiently loaded. The displayed grade is qualified accordingly; repeat under representative use and compare with other evidence. ICMP measures responsiveness to the selected numeric target, which can differ from the LibreSpeed endpoint. Upload reuses the validated download endpoint when download completes cleanly without unrecovered endpoint failure; if upload must run discovery independently (for example after a download discovery or endpoint failure), discovery can select a different endpoint for upload.

Coverage also has a bias in the opposite direction: a link with severe bufferbloat is more likely to return "Grade unavailable" than a D or F grade. Load probes are only attempted while recent transfer counters are increasing, and both the probe-recency and bracketing gates allow at most 0.75 seconds since the last observed counter update, while child activity is reported about every 0.2 seconds. A congestion stall longer than that window stops further probes and shortens the measured duration; a stalled load worker then fails within the two-second socket timeout. The sustained-transfer, five-second counter-growth, 75% valid-probe and three-sample eligibility requirements are consequently harder to satisfy exactly on the worst-behaved links, so severe bufferbloat tends to present as an unavailable grade with its qualification reasons rather than as a low grade.

An individual connection-setup timeout does not automatically disqualify a direction when the remaining workers alone have at least five seconds of observed activity, 1 MiB transferred and accepted, and the required three replies and 75% probe coverage while their counters were increasing. The grade explicitly notes the timeout. Worker accounting must be complete and consistent; any other worker error, complete endpoint failure, missing survivor coverage, child failure, cancellation, deadline, or unclean shutdown still disqualifies the affected direction. The thresholds and saturation qualification above are unchanged.

The automated tests use simulated transports and child processes, not the user's connection. Native interactive testing is still required on Linux, Windows and macOS, including endpoint discovery, realistic load, ICMP availability, display/layout, cancellation in discovery and transfer, and window shutdown. Python 3.11–3.14 and native packaged launches also require testing on each supported environment.
